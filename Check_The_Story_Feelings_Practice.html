Feelings practice

Check the Story

Notice the story → identify the facts → consider alternatives → choose an effective response

Learn to pause between what happened and what your mind says it means

A fact can be directly observed or verified. A story is the meaning, assumption, prediction, or judgment your mind adds. Your story may be accurate, partly accurate, or inaccurate. This practice helps you pause before reacting when certainty is not yet available.

After this practice, you will be able to:

  • Separate facts from interpretations
  • Identify common thinking traps
  • Generate alternative explanations
  • Decide whether action is actually needed
Use this with a current, manageable situation. You do not need to dismiss evidence, tolerate mistreatment, or assume good intentions. If the situation feels too intense, use a coping skill, which is body based, first. Return when it feels manageable.

Common thinking traps

These are patterns to notice—not labels you must memorize.

Mind-reading
Assuming you know what someone thinks.
Predicting the future
Treating a feared outcome as certain.
Assuming intent
Deciding why someone acted without enough evidence.
All-or-nothing
Seeing only total success or total failure.
Overgeneralising
Turning one event into a broad rule.
Personalising
Assuming an event is about you.
Feelings as proof
Treating a feeling as confirmation.
Extreme language
Using always, never, everyone, or nothing.
Round 2 · Independent practice

Try a different situation without step-by-step hints

Check the structure of your answer:
  • What could have been directly observed?
  • What meaning was added?
  • What evidence supports the interpretation—and what does not?
  • What else could explain the event?

Your interpretation may be accurate, partly accurate, or inaccurate. The important step is separating it from what can be confirmed.

Round 3 · Personal application

Apply it to a current situation

Choose one recent situation where you may be reacting to both facts and a story about the facts. You may use a fictional or low-stakes example, keep details private, or leave any optional field blank.

Separate facts from the story

What could a camera, recording, email, document, or witness confirm?

  • Did I include another person’s intention?
  • Did I include a prediction?
  • Did I use always, never, everyone, or completely?
  • Did I describe something that could not be directly observed?

A story may be accurate, partly accurate, or inaccurate. Recognising it as an interpretation does not dismiss it.

Which thinking pattern may be present? (Optional)

Evidence should be specific and observable where possible. A feeling may be important, but the feeling itself does not confirm another person’s intention.

The goal is not the most positive explanation. Choose one that is possible and consistent with the known facts.
