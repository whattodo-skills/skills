Choose an Effective Response

Pause before acting on an emotional urge, identify what matters most, compare realistic responses, and choose one effective next step.

Choose an Effective Response is a guided practice for noticing an emotional urge without automatically acting on it and choosing a response that supports your goals, relationships, values, and self-respect.

Use this skill when anger, fear, shame, sadness, guilt, or another strong emotion is urging you to attack, avoid, withdraw, hide, give in, or act immediately. It can help you pause and choose what is most likely to work instead of reacting only for short-term relief.

Success is not eliminating the emotion or guaranteeing the outcome. Success is noticing the urge, identifying what matters, comparing realistic responses, and choosing one specific action.

Ready to begin?

About 5–7 minutes

Step 1 of 5

1. Pause

Choose one specific situation. You may keep the details brief.

What emotion are you feeling?
What does the emotion urge you to do?
How strong is the urge?

Step 2 of 5

2. Prioritise

Choose a goal within your influence. Example: “State my concern clearly and make a specific request.”

What does this relationship require?

Maintaining a relationship does not always mean giving in.

Which matters most in this situation right now?

Step 3 of 5

3. Generate

What would you do if you acted directly on the urge?

What could help you avoid acting impulsively while the emotion is strong?

What action could address the situation while supporting your priorities?

Which response would give the fastest emotional relief?
Which response is most likely to work over time?

Immediate relief and longer-term effectiveness are not always the same.

Show an example

Situation: A colleague criticises me during a meeting.

Emotion: Anger and embarrassment.

Urge: Send an angry message immediately.

Objective: Correct the misunderstanding and prevent it from happening again.

Relationship: Ongoing collaboration matters.

Self-respect: Speak honestly without insulting the colleague.

Responses: Send the message now. Pause and regulate. Request a private conversation using specific facts.

Comparison: Sending the message may provide immediate relief but could create a larger conflict. Pausing may reduce impulsivity but does not address the issue by itself. A private conversation may feel uncomfortable but is more likely to support the goal, relationship, and self-respect.

Chosen response: Pause, then request a private conversation using specific facts.

This instructional example is never saved as your personal practice.

Step 4 of 5

4. Compare

Choose the two most realistic responses.
What kind of support does this situation need most?

Step 5 of 5

5. Act

Describe something observable and within your control. Example: Take three slow breaths, state the facts, and ask for time to finish my explanation.

If the urge returns, I will:
After I act, I will ask:
  • Did the response support my goal?
  • Did it handle the relationship effectively?
  • Did it preserve self-respect?
  • What would I adjust next time?

This is a future reflection prompt. You do not need to complete it now.

You have considered enough for this practice when you have:

  • Named your main goal
  • Considered the relationship and self-respect
  • Compared two realistic responses
  • Chosen one specific, proportionate next step

The response does not have to guarantee the outcome.
