Choose how to begin

When you are upset, what happened can quickly get mixed together with assumptions, predictions, emotions, urges, past frustrations, and stress. This practice helps you separate those parts before deciding what to do.

Solve the facts when the facts are the problem. Use a different skill when the main difficulty is an assumption, emotional intensity, an ineffective urge, or a reality that cannot be changed.

Choose a current situation that feels manageable enough to examine. You may use a fictional example, keep your answers private, or leave the practice at any time.

Pause or leave

1. Choose

Choose one current, specific situation. You do not need to use personal or traumatic material.

Too broad: Everything at work is a mess.
Specific: My supervisor assigned me another project while I already have two deadlines this week.

Worked example

Situation: My supervisor assigned me another project while I already have two deadlines this week.

Facts: My supervisor assigned me a third project. The three projects have deadlines within the same week. No priority order was provided.

Assumptions: My supervisor does not care whether I succeed. I am being set up to fail.

Another possibility: My supervisor may not know how much work is already scheduled or may expect me to ask for priorities.

Emotions and urges: I feel frustrated and anxious. My urge is to complain to a colleague and avoid starting the new project.

Vulnerability factors: I slept poorly and had a difficult client call earlier in the day.

What is affected: My ability to decide how to allocate my time and complete the highest-priority work.

Real problem: The real problem is that I have three projects with overlapping deadlines and no confirmed priority order, which is affecting my ability to plan and complete the work.

Decision: The facts create a practical problem. Problem Solving is appropriate. The assumption about the supervisor’s intentions can be checked separately.

Next useful step: I will ask my supervisor to confirm the priority order and discuss whether any deadline can be adjusted.

2. Separate

A. Observable facts

Record what was directly seen, heard, received, said, or done. Could a camera or recording capture it? Remove unobserved intentions, predictions, and words like always or never.

B. Interpretations and predictions

The purpose is not to prove the original assumption wrong. It is to recognise that an interpretation is different from a confirmed fact.

C. Emotions and action urges

D. Vulnerability factors and spillover

Examples: fatigue, hunger, illness, discomfort, outside stress, an earlier conflict, a similar past experience, or several problems at once.

Setting a concern aside means addressing it separately, not dismissing it.

3. Define

The facts are affecting:

A strong statement is fact-based, limited to one issue, free from blame, specific enough to address, and connected to a concrete impact.

The real problem is that __________, which is affecting __________.

5. Act

Choose one small action that matches the problem you defined. Possibilities include gathering information, clarifying expectations, making a request, restating a boundary, scheduling a conversation, breaking down a task, testing an assumption, regulating, accepting, separating concerns, or asking for support.

No action will run automatically.
