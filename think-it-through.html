Feelings practice

Think It Through

Pause → Check → Generate → Compare → Choose

Make a more informed decision

Strong emotions can make one explanation feel certain and one response feel urgent. Think It Through helps you pause, examine what you know, consider other possibilities, compare realistic responses, and choose a proportionate next step.

The goal is not to eliminate all uncertainty or guarantee a perfect outcome. The goal is to make a more informed decision.

After this activity you will be able to:

  • Separate observable facts from interpretations.
  • Generate at least two plausible explanations and three possible responses.
  • Compare the likely short- and long-term effects of realistic options.
  • Select one response aligned with the facts, your priorities, and an acceptable level of risk.
  • Plan one immediate action and identify when to reevaluate.

Choose a current decision that feels manageable enough to examine. You may use a provided example, a fictional situation, or keep your answers private.

If the situation requires immediate emergency or professional intervention, use the appropriate support instead of delaying action to complete this exercise.

If your emotional intensity is too high to think through the steps, use a grounding, body-based, or distress-tolerance skill first. Return when the process feels manageable.

Pause

Decision: Whether to resign immediately after receiving the feedback.

Check

Facts: My manager sent me brief feedback identifying two parts of my work that need revision.

Interpretation: My manager thinks I am incompetent and wants me gone.

Other realistic explanations:

  1. My manager may be giving direct feedback because the work needs revision.
  2. The message may have been brief because my manager was busy.
  3. My manager may expect me to ask questions or submit a revised version.

Feared outcome: I will fail, lose my job, or continue being treated as though my work has no value.

Felt likelihood: Very likely
Evidence-based likelihood: Possible

Generate

  1. Resign immediately.
  2. Send an angry response.
  3. Ask what successful revision would look like.
  4. Wait until tomorrow and then decide what response is needed.

Compare

Response A: Ask what successful revision would look like.

Might improve: Clarity about expectations and the quality of the revision.

Might make worse: It may feel uncomfortable to ask for clarification.

Main risk: The conversation may still confirm that there is a larger performance concern.

Response B: Wait until tomorrow and then decide what response is needed.

Might improve: Emotional intensity may decrease enough to think more clearly.

Might make worse: Waiting too long could delay necessary work.

Main risk: I could use the pause to avoid responding rather than to prepare.

Choose

Main priority: Understand what is required before making a major employment decision.

Balanced response: Wait until tomorrow, review the feedback again, and ask my manager to clarify the expected revision.

First action: Draft two specific questions about the feedback.

Reevaluation trigger: Reevaluate after receiving clarification or if additional information shows that the situation is more serious than the original message indicated.

Try a neutral example

You receive a brief message from a colleague saying, “We need to talk about the project.”

Phase 1 of 5

1. Pause

Choose one specific decision. Avoid trying to solve several unrelated problems at the same time.

See examples of a workable scope
  • Whether to respond to a difficult message today
  • Whether to raise a concern with a manager
  • Whether to accept or decline a request
  • Whether to change a plan
  • Whether to take one reversible first step

Too broad: What should I do with my entire life?

Do I need to act immediately?
Does this require emergency, legal, medical, financial, workplace, or other professional support?
If immediate support is required, use that support now. You do not need to complete this exercise first.
What does the emotion urge me to do?

The emotion may contain important information. Naming the urge does not require following it.

Phase 2 of 5

2. Check

Include what was directly observed, said, written, received, documented, or otherwise verified.

  • Could a recording, message, document, or witness confirm this?
  • Did I include someone else’s intention?
  • Did I include a prediction?
  • Did I treat a feeling as proof?

This may be accurate, partly accurate, or inaccurate. For now, identify it separately from what is confirmed.

These do not need to be positive. They only need to be plausible and consistent with what is known.

Felt likelihood: —
Evidence-based likelihood: —

The purpose is not to calculate a perfect probability. Notice whether emotional certainty and available evidence point to the same conclusion.

Phase 3 of 5

3. Generate

Include responses that are realistic and within your control. At least one response should be smaller, delayed, reversible, or information-seeking where appropriate.

See possible response types

Ask for clarification; wait before responding; gather information; state a boundary; take one reversible step; ask for support; decline; accept with conditions; regulate first; do nothing right now; or seek professional advice.

Choose the two most realistic responses to compare.

Select exactly two. Nothing you enter here will carry out an action.

Phase 4 of 5

4. Compare

Optional deeper comparison

Which response is easier to reverse or adjust later?

When uncertainty is high, a smaller or more reversible first step may be useful when it does not create a greater safety or ethical risk.

Phase 5 of 5

5. Choose

A balanced response considers what the emotion is communicating, what the facts indicate, what consequences are likely, and what action is effective.

Make the first step specific, realistic, and within your control.

You have considered enough for this practice when you have:

  • Identified the known facts.
  • Considered at least two plausible explanations.
  • Compared two realistic responses.
  • Identified the most important risk.
  • Chosen one proportionate or reversible next step.

You do not need perfect certainty before finishing.

Success check

“Not yet” does not block completion. This checks the process, not whether your decision is correct.
